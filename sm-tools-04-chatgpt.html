What it actually is

Not a caption machine

In a working social media team this tool does four separate jobs, and only one of them is writing. It plans, it reads data, it drafts, and it argues with you before you spend money on something. The people who get promoted using it are usually doing the first two.

The pattern is the same every time. You give it context, you ask for something specific, you check what comes back, and you decide.

STEP 01

Set context

Load the brand, the audience, and the files once, in a workspace you reuse.

STEP 02

Ask specifically

Name the reader, the goal, the format, and what to avoid.

STEP 03

Check

Verify every number, name, and claim before any of it leaves your desk.

STEP 04

Decide

Choose what ships. The tool has no stake in whether it was right.

Steps one, three, and four are yours. That is not a limitation of the tool, it is the shape of the job, and the gap between someone who is useful with this and someone who is not lives almost entirely in step three.

It will answer anything. That is the feature and the danger in the same sentence.

Do this first

Set up a workspace before you set up a habit

The difference between someone who uses this well and someone who does not is usually not prompting skill. It is that one of them stopped starting from zero every morning.

Four things to load once

Your standing context

  • 01
    Who you are talking to. A real description of your audience, not a demographic. What they are worried about and what they already tried.
  • 02
    What you sound like. A sample of your genuine best posts, plus a short list of words your organization never uses. The banned list does more work than the style description.
  • 03
    What is off limits. Claims you cannot make, topics you avoid, and anything requiring approval. Written down once so nobody has to remember it at eleven at night.
  • 04
    One account per workspace. If you run several, keep them separated so one brand's voice stops leaking into another's drafts.

This takes under an hour and pays back the first week. It is also the thing that turns a tool you occasionally poke at into something a team can actually rely on.

Careers

Who uses this and for what

This tool shows up in all four lanes, and the reason is different in each. That distinction is worth having ready when someone asks how you use AI in an interview.

Creative

Getting unstuck

Forty hooks in a minute so you are choosing rather than staring. The value you add is recognizing which one sounds like a person said it.

Strategic

Pressure testing

Asking it to argue against your plan and list how it could be misread, before a campaign is expensive to change. Cheap rehearsal for an expensive decision.

Analytical

Reading the export

Turning a messy performance file into a finding in minutes. This is where the tool most changes how you are seen, because it moves you from reporting numbers to explaining them.

Business

Follow up and prep

Drafting the message after the meeting while it still matters, and researching a partner before the call. Speed on the unglamorous parts wins deals.

Worth saying plainly: everyone applying for the job next to you also uses this. Listing it means nothing on its own. What distinguishes you is being able to describe a specific workflow you built and what you check before anything ships.

The honest version

What it is not good at

This tool has the broadest feature set of anything in the course, which makes its failures harder to see. Bad design looks bad. Wrong writing looks fine.

01
Telling you when it is guessingThere is no tone change between a sourced fact and an invented one. It cannot flag which parts of its own output it is least sure about, which is why checking has to be a habit rather than a reaction.
02
Knowing anything about your worldIt has no idea who follows you, what happened at your organization last month, or why a phrase would land badly with your community. Everything specific to you has to be supplied by you.
03
Sounding unlike everyone elseIt has a default voice, and audiences have learned to recognize it. Reading a draft out loud is still the fastest filter, and the second fastest is a list of words you refuse to use.
04
Caring whether it was rightIt has no stake in the outcome. You are the only party in the conversation who is accountable for what gets published, and that asymmetry never changes.
05
Replacing the thinkingIt is extraordinary at producing options and poor at knowing which one matters. If you have not decided what you want, it will confidently help you build the wrong thing faster.
Practical notes

Four things worth knowing

Free tiers move constantly

Which features sit behind which plan changes several times a year. Check what your account actually has before planning a workflow around a feature you read about.

Features get removed too

This category adds capabilities loudly and retires them quietly. Building a process that depends entirely on one feature is a risk worth knowing you are taking.

Never paste anything confidential

Student records, health information, unreleased plans, and anything covered by a privacy policy stay out. Check your organization's rules before uploading a file, not after.

Disclosure rules still apply

Using it to draft does not usually require a label. Publishing something it fabricated does require a correction, which is a much worse conversation.

Feature names, plan tiers, and availability on this platform change frequently. Confirm anything specific on the vendor's own site before building a budget or a lesson around it.
